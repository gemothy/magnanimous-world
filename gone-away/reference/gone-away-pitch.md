# GONE AWAY

A first-person mystery at a luxury island resort frozen in its 1960s prime, where the plumber
hired to fix the water uncovers who really owns paradise.

Narrative mystery / atmospheric exploration · 3–4 hours · PC · Solo developer · Unreal Engine 5

## Overview

Gone Away is a compact, atmosphere-driven mystery in the tradition of Knives Out and The White
Lotus: a closed circle of wealthy suspects, a dead body, and an outsider who sees the estate
from below.

The player is a plumber pulled out of the modern world — contract work, airport lounges, a phone
that never stops — and flown to a private Caribbean island to repair the failing water system of
the Magnanimis Resort. The island is a genuine dead zone: no tower, no cable, nothing. It always
has been — that is why it stayed forgotten, and why Garmus could build what he built here. The
new owners simply rebranded the fact: the Magnanimis is marketed as the world's most exclusive
digital detox, and guests surrender their devices at the jetty anyway — a ritual that has less to
do with connectivity than with cameras. The player's phone goes into the same velvet drawer. One
satellite phone exists on the island, in the manager's office, behind doors the plumbing work
will eventually reach.

Inside, it is permanently 1965. The resort was built in the tiki era and preserved in it —
carved mahogany, torchlight, exotica on the original turntables — kept with a fidelity that is
part brand, part devotion, and a little past its prime. The paint is perfect but the empire
underneath is not: the water is failing, and the contract says the player doesn't leave until it
runs.

The investigation and the repair work are the same activity. Every line the player restores
opens another part of the island, and the answer to the mystery is physically located inside the
system they were hired to fix.

There is no combat, no timer, and no fail state. The game is designed to be lingered in, on the
pacing model of Paradise Killer and Outer Wilds: the player investigates at their own speed and
commits to an accusation only when they choose to.

## Story

The Magnanimis was built in the early 1960s by Garmus Campoza, a self-made drifter who arrived
on the island with nothing and became something between a hotelier and a spiritual teacher. His
resort was a sanctuary — for the islanders first, then for the jet set, who came for the glamour
and stayed for the man. For a decade it was the most talked-about secret in the Caribbean. That
decade is everywhere: in the photographs on the walls, in the menus that haven't changed, in the
stories the older guests still tell.

Garmus disappeared. No body was found. The locals believe he walked into the sea. A court on
another island declared him dead in absentia, the estate was forced into sale, and — after
decades of decline and a string of owners — a private equity group acquired the resort at
auction. The Group is restoring the 1965 shine at enormous cost, because the era is the product:
analog paradise for people exhausted by the present. Phase two is the island's freshwater
spring, to be bottled as the flagship of a wellness brand.

That legal chain is the story's engine. The Group's title depends entirely on the old death
ruling. If Garmus is alive, the sale is void. If he was murdered, the sale collapses under
investigation. The only version of the past the owners can afford is an accident — and when a
guest who has been quietly re-examining that ruling is found drowned in the spring-fed pool,
every person on the island becomes a suspect with the same motive: the deed.

No one can call out. No one can look anything up. The island runs on paper, memory, and pipe.
The player works both mysteries at once — the drowned guest and the vanished founder — through
conversation with the guests and staff, and through physical access to the water infrastructure
that no one else can read. The two mysteries converge underground.

## Cast

- **Margaret Von Rotter** — old European money, and old enough to have summered here in the
  prime. The only guest who knew Garmus personally, and the only one who understood what the
  island was worth before anyone else did.
- **Lionel Ocean** — the PE partner who championed the acquisition to his fund. If the deal
  unravels, so does he.
- **Jonathan Harrellson** — Margaret's fixer. Polished, underestimated, and suddenly in
  possession of papers worth more than his salary.
- **Shauntess DeMonta** — a Spanish aristocrat and Garmus's former lover. Her memories of the
  golden years are the player's only firsthand account of the man — vivid, and unreliable.
- **Mahmut bin Sakin** — an energy magnate positioning himself for the water rights, whoever
  ends up holding the resort.
- **Lisa Karpacivoc** — an oligarch's daughter who found one of Garmus's texts in a dead man's
  library, and came because one line in it was true. She knows the prime only from photographs.

The islanders form a second layer. They still maintain Garmus's original systems with exact,
ritualized fidelity, and they are unfailingly warm to the player. Whether that warmth is
hospitality or observation is one of the game's running questions.

## Design Pillars

**Repair is investigation.** Progress comes from doing the job, with the analog tools the resort
insists on: pressure gauges, dye tests, a listening rod held against sixty-year-old pipe.
Valves, pumps, and maintenance passages gate the island's spaces; each repair changes what the
player can reach and what the residents are willing to say.

**The resort is a social instrument.** Guests keep schedules and move between locations. Sitting
down — at the bar, by the firepit, at the pool — is an interaction: it settles the scene and puts
nearby conversation in play. Information is gathered by being present, not by dialogue menus
alone.

**The prime is a discoverable layer.** The 1960s golden age exists in the world as physical
record — photographs, guest books, menus, home movies, and Garmus's personal vinyl collection,
still shelved in the lounge. Playing a dead man's records in his own room is both the game's
collectible loop and its emotional core. The player never visits the prime; they stand in what's
left of it.

**Player-owned deduction.** Nothing auto-solves. The player maintains their own case board and
can close the mystery with an incomplete or wrong theory. The full truth about Garmus is
discoverable but never forced; thorough players find a materially different ending.

**The ending is the job.** The finale is not a dialogue choice. With the system finally under the
player's control, they decide how to restore it — to the Group's specification, to Garmus's
original design, or a third configuration the game never names directly. Each is executed
through the same repair mechanics used throughout.

## Tone and Audio

Nighttime tropics, firelight and torchlight against dark water, tiki architecture preserved just
past its prime — immaculate at a glance, tired at the seams. The soundtrack is surf rock and
lounge exotica sourced from the world itself: Garmus's collection, found and played by the player
on the resort's original equipment.

## Market Position

The comparison set — Paradise Killer, Return of the Obra Dinn, Outer Wilds, Gone Home —
demonstrates a durable audience for short, dense, author-driven mysteries, and each of those
titles succeeded on a small team or a single developer. Gone Away differentiates on setting and
register: the detective-fiction structure of Knives Out and the wealth satire of The White Lotus
have large mainstream audiences and no direct equivalent in games, and the digital-detox premise
gives the closed-circle mystery a contemporary reason to exist.

Scope is deliberately contained: one resort, a single connected map, eight speaking characters,
3–4 hours of content built for a strong screenshot-and-clip presence. The aesthetic is the
marketing.
